October 20, 2026 · Ricardo Casanova

AI Company Honesty

Something peculiar is happening in the artificial intelligence industry.

Some of the world’s most powerful AI companies are voluntarily publishing increasingly detailed accounts of their own technology behaving unpredictably, circumventing safeguards, exploiting vulnerabilities, and occasionally concealing its actions. They’re doing so without necessarily being compelled by regulators, government investigations, or outside scrutiny.

I’ve dabbled in politics, risk management, public relations, and corporate communications over the years. Enough to appreciate that when a multibillion-dollar organization makes a potentially damaging public statement, the decision is rarely spontaneous. Corporate communications are carefully considered, with legal, financial, reputational, and political implications weighed before anything reaches the public. These companies employ sophisticated communications professionals, lawyers, and strategic advisers whose responsibilities include protecting corporate interests and managing how information is presented.

So when companies like OpenAI and Anthropic begin voluntarily telling the world that their technology is doing things they didn’t anticipate, I have a fairly straightforward question.

What’s the angle?

I find it difficult to believe corporate altruism has suddenly become fashionable in Silicon Valley.

Something changed in 2026

AI companies have published safety research for years. System cards, theoretical risk assessments, controlled experiments, and warnings about what increasingly capable AI might do were already part of the industry’s communications. Much of that reporting concerned hypothetical scenarios or behaviors observed under controlled conditions.

Then the reporting began to change.

On February 27, 2026, the Trump administration ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic following a confrontation over military applications of its technology. In June, the US government imposed export restrictions on Anthropic’s Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models, temporarily forcing the company to suspend access. The disputes demonstrated how government intervention could directly threaten the commercial operations of a frontier AI company.

In July, OpenAI began publicly addressing specific security incidents. On September 16, it introduced a formal framework for reporting model misalignment, acknowledging that its previous disclosures had been irregular and relatively infrequent. On October 9, Anthropic explicitly announced an effort to publish more frequent standalone reports detailing unintended model behavior.

The distinction matters. These reports increasingly describe specific incidents, identifiable actions, compromised safeguards, and the circumstances surrounding failures. Agents exploiting software vulnerabilities, circumventing restrictions, accessing external systems, and interfering with monitoring mechanisms are no longer confined to broad discussions about theoretical risks.

The frequency, specificity, and implications of the disclosures appear to have shifted substantially, and the companies themselves have acknowledged changing their reporting practices.

We cannot establish that government intervention caused this shift, plus increasingly autonomous systems and improvements in detecting problematic behavior offer plausible explanations. Nevertheless, the chronology is difficult to ignore. Within months of governments demonstrating their willingness to interfere directly with frontier AI businesses, those same businesses were formalizing more detailed and frequent public disclosures of their own failures.

From a political risk management perspective, voluntary transparency could provide an effective defense against future intervention.

Establishing authority over the problem

Scenario today: An AI company develops increasingly autonomous technology and it, in turn, behaves unpredictably. The company identifies the problem, investigates it, publishes its findings, develops safeguards, and proposes reporting standards.

The company becomes the developer, investigator, technical authority, and potentially the architect of the industry’s safety framework.

It also addresses an obvious criticism: If you’re part of the problem, why should you be trusted to be part of the solution?

By voluntarily exposing problems, these companies establish credibility as the organizations most qualified to address them. Governments seek their expertise, enterprise customers seek assurances, and investors see evidence of responsible governance.

The companies remain indispensable to the very process intended to hold them accountable. Following a September 29 meeting with leading AI executives, Trump praised what he called “tremendous self-policing” and endorsed a voluntary industry agreement. While the agreement included provisions for external review, the administration’s willingness to rely heavily on industry-led oversight illustrates precisely why establishing credibility as a responsible developer carries political and commercial value.

By positioning themselves as responsible, self-regulating organizations capable of policing their own technology, these companies are effectively making the case to be handed the keys to industry oversight. If governments adopt their frameworks and require extensive safety evaluations, monitoring infrastructure, specialized compliance personnel, and formal incident reporting, the largest AI companies already have much of that infrastructure in place. Smaller competitors would face enormous compliance costs, and the barrier to entry would skyrocket. The industry’s dominant players could end up helping write the rules, policing compliance, and reinforcing their own market dominance in the process.

Industry participation in regulation is necessary, but allowing organizations with substantial financial interests to dominate the development of those rules creates obvious conflicts.

The companies that help define responsible AI development may also influence who can afford to participate in the market.

The indemnification play

Voluntary transparency may also help establish the foundations for transferring financial responsibility.

Imagine an AI company publishes extensive documentation describing the risks associated with its autonomous systems. It warns customers about unexpected behavior, security vulnerabilities, and potential operational failures. It also develops monitoring systems, enterprise safeguards, compliance infrastructure, and additional security controls to mitigate those risks.

Some safeguards may be included in the product. Others could require substantial additional expenditure.

Now imagine a corporate customer suffers a serious financial loss involving an autonomous AI agent. The customer argues that the technology behaved unpredictably, while the vendor points to years of disclosures, recommended safeguards, deployment guidelines, and contractual provisions allocating responsibility.

Did the customer implement the recommended safeguards? Were the monitoring tools enabled? Were deployment procedures followed?

If the answer to any of those questions is no, the AI company may argue that the customer failed to take reasonable precautions.

OpenAI’s October 2026 service terms already exclude certain intellectual-property indemnification protections when customers disable, ignore, or fail to use relevant safety features. The provision concerns a specific category of intellectual-property claims and does not automatically transfer liability for unrelated incidents. Nevertheless, it demonstrates how the availability and use of safeguards can influence contractual responsibility.

Public disclosures alone don’t eliminate legal liability. Responsibility depends on applicable law, contractual provisions, the circumstances of an incident, and the conduct of the parties involved.

However, documented warnings, recommended mitigation measures, and contractual limitations can form part of a broader strategy for managing financial exposure.

Could today’s voluntary safety disclosures become tomorrow’s evidence that customers were adequately warned?

An AI company could potentially earn revenue selling autonomous technology, generate additional revenue providing tools to manage its risks, and rely on documented warnings and contractual protections to limit its exposure when those risks materialize.

That arrangement would be commercially attractive, particularly as autonomous systems assume greater responsibility for financially consequential decisions and operations.

Normalizing the abnormal

The increasingly frequent disclosures may also be desensitizing us to AI behavior that would once have caused considerable alarm. Agents circumventing security restrictions, exploiting vulnerabilities, and interfering with their own monitoring systems are becoming routine subjects of corporate research reports. With each disclosure, the extraordinary becomes a little more ordinary, and the public grows accustomed to the idea that unpredictable behavior is simply part of developing advanced AI.

That raises some uncomfortable questions: What aren’t they telling us? Are these isolated incidents, or carefully selected examples of a much larger problem? Are they preparing us for more serious failures they already know about, or anticipating what increasingly autonomous systems might do next? We have no evidence that they’re deliberately withholding something worse, but the possibility deserves scrutiny. From a communications and risk management perspective, establishing public awareness of known risks before a major incident occurs has obvious value. When something more consequential happens, the companies can point to years of warnings, published research, and recommended safeguards.

If the public has already been conditioned to expect unpredictable AI behavior, the next major failure may be easier to explain, easier to defend, and ultimately less damaging to the companies responsible.

Follow the money

These companies compete for enormous amounts of capital, market share, and influence. The legal dispute between Elon Musk and OpenAI illustrated the tensions between stated missions, corporate governance, and commercial ambitions. Whatever their organizational structures or public commitments, frontier AI companies face immense pressure to generate revenue, attract investment, and protect their market positions.

Voluntary safety disclosures can serve those interests by reassuring enterprise customers, establishing credibility with regulators, influencing industry standards, and creating demand for additional monitoring and compliance services. They may also strengthen contractual defenses, reduce financial exposure, and raise barriers to entry for smaller competitors. These are tangible commercial advantages, whether the disclosures were designed to achieve them or not.

None of this requires a coordinated strategy or dishonest intentions. Genuine safety concerns and commercial self-interest can coexist, but the financial incentives are too significant to ignore when evaluating why these companies have changed how, when, and what they disclose.

The real test of transparency

I don’t doubt that many AI researchers genuinely want to make these systems safer, or that publishing incidents benefits the broader research community. My concern is that the organizations developing these technologies also control much of the information used to evaluate them. They determine which incidents warrant disclosure, how serious those incidents are, and what remains confidential. Without meaningful independent oversight, we’re largely relying on the companies themselves to tell us how much we should be concerned about their products.

The shift in disclosure practices during 2026 is significant, particularly given its timing alongside government intervention in the industry. Whether this reflects a genuine commitment to greater accountability, a calculated effort to protect commercial interests, or some combination of the two remains unclear. What matters is that these companies stand to benefit from defining the problems, influencing the standards, selling the safeguards, and potentially limiting their financial responsibility when things go wrong. I’d be interested to see whether their enthusiasm for transparency extends to disclosures that could seriously damage their own businesses.

The real measure of corporate transparency isn’t what a company willingly reveals when disclosure benefits its business. It’s what that company is prepared to reveal when telling the truth could cost it money.

Until independent oversight can answer that question, I’ll keep following the money.
