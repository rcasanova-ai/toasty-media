AI: The Copycat Economy

What happens when AI makes creating and copying equally accessible?

For decades, the conventional wisdom in business has been to build in stealth. Protect your ideas, keep your plans close, don’t reveal too much about what you’re working on, and certainly don’t give your competitors an opportunity to see what you’re doing before you’re ready to launch. It’s an approach that has made sense for a long time, particularly when developing a product required significant capital, specialized expertise, and months or years of execution.

But I’m beginning to question how much sense that approach makes in a world where artificial intelligence is fundamentally changing the economics of creation. When someone can take an idea that previously required a team of developers and months of work and produce a functional prototype in a matter of days, what exactly are we protecting by keeping everything behind closed doors?

I don’t think we’ve fully appreciated how profoundly artificial intelligence is going to change the way businesses are created and operated. Much of the public conversation still revolves around whether AI will replace particular jobs, but the more interesting development, at least from an entrepreneurial perspective, is how dramatically it’s reducing the resources required to execute an idea.

AI is changing the economics of building

McKinsey’s 2025 State of AI research found that 88% of surveyed organizations were using AI in at least one business function. Meanwhile, research published by Microsoft examining 4,867 software developers across three companies found that developers with access to AI coding assistants completed approximately 26% more tasks than those without access.

[Sources: McKinsey, The State of AI 2025; Microsoft Research, AI Coding Assistants and Developer Productivity]

Those numbers are meaningful, but I suspect they’re only an early indication of what’s coming. We’re moving toward an environment where individuals and small teams can accomplish things that previously required entire departments. Software development, research, design, marketing, financial modeling, and increasingly complex operational processes are becoming accessible to people who would never have had the technical expertise or financial resources to attempt them before.

Personally, I find that enormously exciting. I’ve spent much of my career developing ideas, building relationships, and trying to connect opportunities with the people and resources needed to execute them. The ability to move from concept to something tangible without assembling an enormous team fundamentally changes what’s possible. I have had dozens of ideas I wanted to get off the ground but didn't have a spare $10k for a sub quality product from a sub quality developer.

But there’s an uncomfortable consequence to all of this. The same technology that makes it easier for someone to create something original also makes it easier for somebody else to imitate it.

When creating and copying become equally accessible

I’ve been following some of the developments around AI-assisted reverse engineering of video games, particularly projects involving classic Nintendo titles and experiments with PlayStation software. The ability to analyze existing games, reconstruct their functionality, and potentially make them available on platforms they were never intended to run on is fascinating from a technical perspective.

Whether I agree with it, or quietly enjoy seeing someone give the middle finger to a large video game publisher, is another discussion entirely. There’s a legitimate debate about preservation, ownership, consumer rights, and the degree of control publishers should retain over software people have purchased.

But what concerns me more is what happens when similar capabilities are directed toward independent creators who don’t have enormous legal departments or the financial resources to defend themselves.

In September 2026, a developer associated with the indie game Iron Pilgrim publicly alleged that someone had recreated the game using AI and demanded $200 to prevent a competing version from being released on Steam. The story attracted attention because it suggested a particularly unpleasant form of digital extortion: someone threatening to reproduce and commercially exploit a creator’s work unless they were paid.

Subsequent reporting raised serious questions about the circumstances of that particular allegation, so it shouldn’t be treated as a verified example of a widespread criminal practice. Nevertheless, the concern it brought into focus is worth discussing. As AI-assisted development and reverse engineering improve, the cost of imitating existing creative work may continue to fall.

[Source: AUTOMATON, October 2026]

Of course, people have been stealing ideas and copying products for as long as commerce has existed. What’s changing is the potential speed, accessibility, and scale of that behavior. A competitor might be able to reproduce much of your software without understanding the circumstances that led to its creation, the relationships that support it, or the reasons customers chose it in the first place.

What happens when the technology stops being the advantage?

Over the next several years, I expect we’ll see an enormous increase in the number of products and businesses being created. AI will continue to lower development costs, accelerate experimentation, and allow individuals to compete in markets that previously required substantial financial and technical resources.

We’ll also see more imitation, more competition, and an increasingly difficult environment for businesses whose primary advantage is simply having developed a particular set of features.

If everyone can build faster, everyone can copy faster too. That doesn't make innovation pointless, but it does force us to reconsider what makes a business defensible. Perhaps the more interesting question is what competitors cannot reproduce quite so easily: the relationships, reputation, judgment, and history behind the product. That's where I think the next competitive advantage begins.
