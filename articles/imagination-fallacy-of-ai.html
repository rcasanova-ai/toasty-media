The Imagination Fallacy of AI

For a while, I genuinely worried about what artificial intelligence would mean for the creative arts. Writing, music, filmmaking, video games - storytelling. These are things I've loved my entire life, and watching AI models become increasingly capable of producing creative content was unsettling. Now, I'm becoming considerably less worried.

The more I watch companies pour billions into increasingly powerful models, generating content at extraordinary speeds, the more I'm reminded of something we've known for decades: Money, technology and production capacity have never guaranteed creative success.

Hollywood has enormous budgets, internationally recognized celebrities, accomplished directors and access to virtually every production resource imaginable. Yet studios still produce spectacularly expensive box office failures. Record labels can put millions behind an artist, hire the best producers, manufacture publicity and still release a song nobody particularly cares about.

If creativity were simply a matter of resources, some of the biggest failures in entertainment history would never have happened and AI doesn't fundamentally change that equation.

Consider video games: I'll happily admit that sometimes I enjoy a good, brainless shooter. There's something satisfying about turning off your brain and blowing things up for a few hours. But the games that have stayed with me throughout my life are different.

The sweeping narratives of Final Fantasy. The extraordinary character development and moral complexity of The Witcher 3. The humanity, tragedy and breathtaking world of Red Dead Redemption 2. The historical atmosphere and personal sacrifice woven throughout Ghost of Tsushima. And, of course, the unforgettable worlds and characters of Mass Effect.

These experiences stay with us because someone imagined them. Someone understood how to make us care about a fictional character, a place that doesn't exist, or a decision that has absolutely no consequence outside a computer screen.And yet, somehow, those experiences can mean something to us years later.

I was reminded of this recently by the news that BioWare was closing its doors. For those unfamiliar, BioWare was founded in Edmonton, Alberta, my hometown. It became one of the most influential role-playing game studios in the world, creating titles like Baldur's Gate, Dragon Age, Knights of the Old Republic and Mass Effect.

Growing up in Edmonton, BioWare was close to home in more ways than one. I knew quite a few people who worked there over the years. Many were writers; funny, imaginative, exceptionally talented people who could construct entire worlds, develop complicated characters and bring stories to life. It's incredibly sad to see that chapter close after more than three decades. But something occurred to me when I read the news.

The studio may be gone, but that creative talent hasn't disappeared. Those people still possess the imagination, experience, humour and storytelling instincts that made their work special. Job hunting in a niche market sucks but wherever they go next, they'll take that richness with them.

I don't believe creativity is some mystical human property that machines will never be able to approximate. AI is already capable of producing remarkable images, music, dialogue and increasingly sophisticated narratives. Those capabilities will continue improving. But producing something technically impressive and creating something people genuinely connect with are very different challenges.

Human creativity draws from a lifetime of experiences. Our relationships, failures, humor, grief, cultural identities, absurdities and contradictions shape how we see the world. Two people can experience the same event and tell entirely different stories about it.

You can teach someone the mechanics of writing, composition or filmmaking. You cannot simply hand them a lifetime of perspective, emotional understanding or creative instinct and that's where AI ultimately makes human creativity even more valuable.

As the volume of generated content explodes, audiences will become increasingly selective about what deserves their attention. When everyone can generate a beautiful image, an orchestral soundtrack or a cinematic sequence in minutes, technical production quality alone becomes a considerably weaker differentiator, it straight up isn't moat. Originality, narrative, perspective and emotional connection become more important. The same principle applies to business, and frankly, I think a lot of companies are missing it.

We're watching organizations throw AI at virtually everything. Customer communications, marketing campaigns, sales outreach, content creation and customer service. The assumption often seems to be that generating more material, contacting more prospects and automating more interactions must somehow translate into better business outcomes.

But what happens when every company can generate the same polished marketing copy, personalized email sequences and carefully optimized customer communications?

What makes anyone pay attention?

Understanding your audience has always mattered: Knowing what motivates people, what frustrates them, what earns their trust and what makes them feel understood is fundamental to building lasting relationships.

AI can help organizations develop that understanding, particularly when it's informed by meaningful customer context and human behaviour. It can support creativity, improve research, uncover patterns and give talented people capabilities they never previously had.

But using AI without understanding the people you're trying to reach is simply a faster way to produce things they may never have wanted in the first place. I think some organizations are heading toward an expensive realization. They've invested heavily in technology without investing nearly enough thought into what they're trying to accomplish, whom they're trying to reach or why anyone should care.

We've seen this movie before. Bigger budgets don't guarantee better stories.

The companies that succeed with AI will be the ones that understand their audiences, recognize the value of human creativity and know where technology genuinely improves the experience. But I don't think the future belongs to whoever can produce the most content at the lowest cost.
