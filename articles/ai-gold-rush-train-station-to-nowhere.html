The AI Gold Rush and the Train Station to Nowhere

Every gold rush leaves behind infrastructure built for a future that never quite arrived. Railway stations, roads and entire towns were constructed on the assumption that prosperity would last. Some became the foundations of thriving communities. Others became monuments to a boom that moved on.

Today's AI infrastructure boom presents governments with a similar question. Data centres can be valuable, long-lived assets. They can also place considerable demands on electricity networks, water supplies and public planning. The critical issue is whether the communities hosting them capture enough lasting economic value to justify those demands.

A headline announcing billions in investment sounds impressive. It tells us very little about who receives the investment, how many permanent jobs remain after construction, what public infrastructure must be upgraded, or who pays if the economics change.

A gold rush with real consequences

Across North America and Europe, proposed data centres have prompted disputes over electricity capacity, land use, water, noise and local benefits. The impacts differ significantly by location and design. A facility using closed-loop cooling and contracted new generation presents a different proposition from one drawing heavily on an already constrained grid. Neither should be judged by headline investment alone.

Ireland illustrates the challenge of balancing digital infrastructure growth with electricity-system constraints. Grid operators and policymakers have scrutinized how additional large electricity users can connect without undermining system reliability. Similar questions are being asked in communities elsewhere, even when the regulatory answers differ.

The debate should not begin with the assumption that every data centre is harmful or that every project is an economic victory. It should begin with transparent evidence: expected peak power demand, cooling design, water use, grid upgrades, permanent employment, tax arrangements and enforceable commitments.

Thailand's opportunity, and its risk

Thailand has a genuine opportunity to attract digital infrastructure and the businesses that grow around it. Regional connectivity, investment and cloud capacity can support domestic companies. But hosting foreign-owned computing infrastructure is not the same thing as building a domestic technology industry.

If the lasting local contribution is limited to land, electricity, construction contracts and a relatively small operations workforce, governments should be cautious about describing a project as a transformation of the national economy. The more important questions are whether Thai businesses gain affordable access to computing, whether local engineers develop transferable skills, and whether suppliers and researchers can participate in the value created.

Those outcomes are possible. They are not automatic. Governments can negotiate for them, measure them and publish the results.

Technology changes. Concrete stays.

AI computing is changing quickly. Models are becoming more efficient in some tasks, while demand for training and inference continues to expand. Smaller models and on-device inference will handle a growing share of workloads, but that does not establish that hyperscale facilities are about to become obsolete. Both centralized and distributed computing are likely to matter.

That uncertainty is exactly why infrastructure planning requires scenarios rather than slogans. What happens if projected utilization is lower than expected? Can a facility be upgraded as hardware changes? Who finances grid improvements? Are contracts structured so the public does not absorb private downside risk?

An infrastructure investment can make sense even when the technology changes. The mistake is assuming that today's forecast is guaranteed, or that every megawatt committed to computing will generate comparable local benefits.

Build an economy, not just a building

The strongest national strategy connects infrastructure approvals to measurable public value. That could include transparent resource reporting, independently assessed grid and water impacts, investment in local suppliers, workforce training tied to actual jobs, and practical access for domestic businesses and researchers.

Education matters here, too. Universities should not chase every short-lived framework, but neither are four-year degrees suddenly useless. Strong foundations in engineering, mathematics, computing and critical thinking remain valuable precisely because tools change. Short courses and employer partnerships can complement that foundation by responding more quickly to new technologies.

A sensible policy would also distinguish speculative announcements from operational results. Publish the commitments. Track what was delivered. Revisit the assumptions when market conditions change.

The station at the end of the line

The railway analogy is not an argument against building railways. It is a reminder that infrastructure is only as valuable as the economy it serves.

AI data centres may become essential parts of tomorrow's digital economy. But governments should be able to explain, in plain numbers, what their citizens receive in return for the land, electricity, water and public support committed today.

Otherwise, the ribbon-cutting ceremony may be remembered longer than the prosperity it was supposed to create.
