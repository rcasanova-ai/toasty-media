The Product Trifecta Fallacy of AI

There’s an old adage in business and project management: fast, cheap or good. Pick two.

For decades, this triangle represented an uncomfortable reality. You could have something quickly and inexpensively, but quality would probably suffer. You could have something inexpensive and exceptionally well made, but it would take time. Or you could have something fast and excellent, provided you were willing to pay for it.

Then along came AI, apparently determined to make a mockery of the entire concept.

Today, you can generate a functioning application in an afternoon, produce professional-looking designs in minutes and accomplish things that would previously have required an entire development team. The cost of producing software has fallen dramatically, and the speed at which we can turn ideas into working prototypes is extraordinary. AI has certainly challenged the traditional relationship between speed, cost and quality, although I think we’re getting a little ahead of ourselves when we declare that relationship obsolete.

More importantly, I think we’ve been looking at the wrong triangle.

There’s another trifecta in product development that I’ve always considered fundamental: product, design and engineering. Each brings something essential to the table, and while their responsibilities frequently overlap, they approach problems from different perspectives.

Product asks whether we’re solving the right problem, who we’re solving it for and whether anyone actually needs what we’re building. Design considers how people experience that solution. Is it intuitive, accessible and enjoyable to use? Engineering determines whether we can build it, whether it works reliably and whether the underlying architecture can support what we’re trying to accomplish.

Take one away, and things start getting interesting. Give a designer complete control, and you might end up with something absolutely beautiful that nobody needs and that engineering struggles to implement. Give a developer complete control, and you might get something technically brilliant that looks like it was assembled in a basement in 1997. It works perfectly, provided you can figure out how to use it. Whether anyone actually needs it is another question. Give a product person complete control, and you’ll probably get an ambitious roadmap, a compelling vision and an endless supply of ideas. Whether any of them become functional, usable products is considerably less certain.

Obviously, talented people regularly cross these boundaries. Some designers are excellent product thinkers. Some developers have extraordinary design instincts. Some product people can write excellent code. But the disciplines exist for a reason. They require different perspectives, different skills and different ways of evaluating success. And now we’re being told AI can replace all three.

I’ve had an opportunity to put that proposition to the test, and it’s been quite an education. I’ve traditionally been the product guy. I can envision what something should do, understand the problems it needs to solve and connect the dots between what people need and what technology might make possible. Design, on the other hand? I couldn’t design a box. I also have a programming background, although most of my hands-on experience dates back about 25 years. I understand data structures, programming methodologies, software architecture and enough of the underlying principles to have reasonably intelligent conversations with developers. Enough to be dangerous, as they say, but certainly not enough to consider myself a modern software engineer.

AI has completely changed what I can accomplish with that combination of experience and limitations. I can take an idea, work through its requirements, generate interfaces, develop functionality, connect databases and deploy working applications. Things that would previously have required several specialists are suddenly within reach. For someone who has spent years imagining products and relying on other people to help bring them to life, that’s an extraordinary development. I genuinely love what this technology has made possible.

However, I’ve also discovered something that doesn’t receive nearly enough attention in conversations about AI replacing entire teams. Just because I can participate in every aspect of building a product doesn’t mean I can effectively manage all those responsibilities simultaneously.

When I’m debugging an application, I’m not thinking about the product roadmap. When I’m troubleshooting deployment issues, I’m not talking to potential customers. When I’m reviewing interfaces for design consistency, I’m not evaluating market opportunities. And when I’m performing quality assurance, fixing broken functionality, reviewing database structures and checking whether the latest changes introduced three new problems, I’m spending my time doing work that would ordinarily be distributed across several disciplines.

Every hour spent doing those things is an hour I’m not spending on the work where I contribute the most value. It’s also exhausting to constantly switch between responsibilities that require completely different ways of thinking. Product strategy requires a different mindset from debugging. Design review requires different attention from software architecture. Customer discovery requires a completely different perspective from chasing down an authentication error.

Multitasking isn’t the cat’s pyjamas. In my experience, it’s often a remarkably effective way to make sure everything receives less attention than it deserves. You can become so occupied with getting everything done that you lose the opportunity to do anything particularly well. Worse, you can spend an entire day being incredibly productive without making meaningful progress on the things that actually matter to the business.

This is where I suspect many organizations are going to encounter problems with their AI strategies. They’ll look at what one person can accomplish with AI and conclude that they no longer need the other people. Why employ designers when AI can generate interfaces? Why employ developers when AI can write code? Why employ product managers when AI can generate requirements, analyze markets and recommend features? On paper, the economics look fantastic, particularly when executives are being encouraged to demonstrate immediate returns on their AI investments.

But there’s a difference between being capable of performing a task and having the expertise, capacity and judgment to perform it well. AI can generate a beautiful interface without understanding whether it’s appropriate for the audience. It can produce functioning code without recognizing every architectural weakness or security vulnerability. It can generate an impressive product roadmap without having a meaningful understanding of the people who are supposed to use the product. And it can confidently produce all three while reinforcing assumptions that should have been challenged.

One of the most valuable aspects of a good product team is the tension between disciplines. A designer challenges the developer when technical convenience compromises the experience. A developer challenges the designer when an elegant concept introduces unnecessary complexity. A product manager challenges both when everyone becomes fascinated with building something that doesn’t actually solve the customer’s problem. Those conversations can be frustrating, and they certainly don’t always make development faster, but they introduce different perspectives and force people to defend their decisions. That process can prevent extraordinarily expensive mistakes.

When one person and an AI model effectively become the entire team, much of that independent scrutiny can disappear. Certainly, AI can challenge assumptions, review code and critique designs. I use it for precisely those purposes. But the quality of those challenges depends heavily on the context, instructions and judgment involved. AI can misunderstand requirements, overlook important considerations and confidently agree with terrible ideas. If you don’t have sufficient expertise to recognize when something is wrong, you may not discover the problem until it becomes considerably more expensive to fix.

There’s also a question of accountability. In a traditional team, responsibilities are distributed among people who specialize in different aspects of the product. They bring their experience to the table, recognize problems within their areas of expertise and take ownership of the outcomes. When one person is responsible for everything, that person also becomes the bottleneck for every decision, every review and every problem that needs attention. AI may accelerate the work being produced, but it doesn’t automatically increase the human capacity required to evaluate and manage that work.

I don’t think the answer is to abandon AI-assisted development or insist that every project requires a traditional team structure. Quite the opposite. I think AI is creating extraordinary opportunities for smaller teams, independent creators and people who previously lacked the resources to bring their ideas to life. It’s lowering barriers that have existed for decades, and I consider that overwhelmingly positive.

But I also think we’re confusing the ability to produce something with the expertise required to produce it well.

A capable product person with AI can accomplish far more than before. So can a talented designer or an experienced developer. Imagine what happens when those people work together, each using AI to amplify their own expertise. The designer can explore and test ideas more quickly, the developer can spend less time on repetitive implementation and the product manager can devote more attention to understanding customers, validating opportunities and making better decisions. The entire team becomes more capable without expecting any individual to master every discipline.

That seems like a considerably more compelling future than expecting one exhausted person to become an entire product organization.

Of course, there will be exceptions. Some people genuinely possess the experience and talent to operate across multiple disciplines, and AI will make those individuals extraordinarily productive. Small applications and early prototypes may not require dedicated specialists at all. I’ve experienced that benefit firsthand. But building a prototype, operating a reliable product and growing a sustainable business introduce very different demands. The fact that someone can accomplish the first doesn’t automatically mean they have the capacity to manage everything that follows.

Which brings me back to the original triangle. Fast, cheap or good. Pick two.

AI has dramatically improved our ability to produce things quickly and inexpensively, and in many cases, it can improve quality too. But quality in product development isn’t simply whether the application runs, whether the interface looks polished or whether the features match the original specifications. It also means solving the right problem, delivering an experience people genuinely value and building something that can survive beyond its first impressive demonstration.

Those things still require attention, judgment, expertise and accountability. They also require time, regardless of how quickly the underlying technology can generate another thousand lines of code.

I suspect we’re going to see plenty of organizations discover this the expensive way. They’ll reduce teams because AI appears capable of handling the workload, only to discover that the remaining employees are overwhelmed, product quality is inconsistent and the anticipated productivity gains aren’t translating into better business outcomes. They’ll have successfully reduced the cost of producing work while creating entirely new costs associated with managing, reviewing and correcting it.

The irony is that the same technology, applied differently, could make their existing teams substantially more effective.

Perhaps the real fallacy isn’t believing AI can replace designers, developers and product managers. It’s believing that replacing their individual tasks somehow eliminates the need for their collective expertise.

I’ve learned that I can build far more with AI than I ever could before, and I have no intention of slowing down. But I’ve also learned that being able to do everything doesn’t mean I should be doing everything. If I’m spending my days debugging, testing, designing and managing deployments, who’s doing the product thinking?
