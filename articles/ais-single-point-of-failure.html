AI’s Single Point of Failure

For the last three years, the AI industry has sold inevitability. Bigger models, bigger valuations, bigger data centres, bigger promises. Capability would keep rising, access would keep expanding, and the winners would be the companies that moved fastest.

Then, in June, two stories exposed how fragile that assumption can be. A U.S. government directive forced Anthropic to disable access to two of its newest models. Separately, a coalition of state attorneys general opened an investigation into OpenAI. The events were different, but together they raised a question enterprises have been avoiding: what happens when the model you depend on becomes unavailable, restricted, or legally exposed?

When the model disappears

On June 12, Anthropic said it had received a U.S. export-control directive requiring it to suspend access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for foreign nationals, including those inside the United States. Anthropic said it could not reliably enforce that restriction immediately, so it disabled the models for everyone. Other Anthropic models remained available.

The stated concern involved the ability to bypass Fable 5 safeguards in ways that could expose software vulnerabilities. Anthropic challenged the proportionality of the response. The government saw a national security problem; Anthropic saw an order with enormous consequences for legitimate users.

There is an important update to that story. On June 30, Anthropic announced that the export controls had been lifted, and it began restoring access on July 1. That resolution does not erase what happened. It demonstrates that access to a critical AI service can change abruptly for reasons outside a customer's control.

For a developer experimenting with a model, that is frustrating. For a company that has built production workflows around it, that is an operational incident. Customers need to know whether a provider can be shut off, what alternatives exist, and what their contracts actually guarantee.

Why Commerce was involved

Export controls are not limited to chips crossing borders. The U.S. Department of Commerce, through the Bureau of Industry and Security, also administers controls involving technology, software, and certain forms of access by foreign nationals. The legal mechanism is therefore not as surprising as its application to access to a hosted frontier model.

This raises difficult questions about how governments define strategic AI capability, how companies verify eligibility, and how international teams can depend on services that may suddenly become subject to nationality-based restrictions. None of those questions is solved by having a smarter model.

OpenAI's different problem

The OpenAI investigation raised a different category of risk. In June, state attorneys general sought information about matters including advertising, user engagement and retention, treatment of minors and seniors, and handling of consumer and health data. OpenAI said it took the concerns seriously and would engage constructively.

An investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing. But it does signal that consumer protection, privacy, product design, and safety are no longer peripheral concerns. As AI systems become tutors, companions, workplace assistants, and decision tools, regulators will examine what companies promised, how products were used, and what safeguards were in place.

This resembles the trajectory of social platforms, except that AI can influence not only what people read but also what they decide and do. The closer a product moves to consequential decisions, the harder it becomes to treat governance as an optional feature.

The single point of failure

The immediate temptation is to turn these events into a contest between model providers. If one service is restricted, move to another. That might solve a short-term access problem, but it misses the bigger lesson.

The model is a dependency. The strategy is the system around it: data governance, vendor risk, workflow design, human review, auditability, fallback planning, access control, legal exposure, and change management. If those pieces are weak, a better model only makes the failure faster.

Enterprises should be asking whether critical workflows can move to a second provider, whether non-U.S. employees and contractors can retain access if rules change, whether sensitive information is appropriately protected, and whether contracts address service interruptions. Most importantly, can the organization prove what the system did, why a decision was made, and who was accountable for approving it?

These are familiar infrastructure questions. Telecommunications networks are designed with redundancy because links fail. Data centres plan for power interruptions because electricity is essential. Financial systems reconcile transactions because mistakes have consequences. AI deserves the same operational discipline if we expect it to become infrastructure.

What happens when it goes dark?

For years, we've been told that AI will become as essential to business as electricity, telecommunications, and the internet. Perhaps that's true. But imagine building your entire business around an electricity provider that could disconnect you tomorrow because of a political dispute, a regulatory decision, or a change in its own commercial priorities.

Nobody would consider that a sensible infrastructure strategy.

Yet that's precisely what we're doing with AI.

The technology is extraordinary. The dependency we're creating around it deserves considerably more scrutiny.

And perhaps the most important question executives should be asking isn't which AI model is the smartest.

It's what happens to their business when that model is no longer available.
