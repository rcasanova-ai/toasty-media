AI: The Human Advantage

Why trust, reputation, and relationships may become our greatest competitive advantage.

We have become extraordinarily good at connecting devices, platforms, and businesses. Yet I wonder whether we have become quite so good at connecting with one another.

That question has been on my mind as I build new projects, attend industry events, and watch artificial intelligence become part of everyday business. AI can help us find people, understand information, and take the administrative burden out of maintaining relationships. But the relationship itself still has to happen between people.

Connected everywhere, close to fewer people

The World Health Organization’s 2025 Commission on Social Connection reported that approximately one in six people worldwide experiences loneliness, rising to roughly one in five among adolescents and young adults. That is a remarkable finding in a world where reaching someone on the other side of the planet takes seconds.

[Source: World Health Organization, Commission on Social Connection, 2025]

I don't think technology created all of that loneliness. But I do think it is worth asking whether the tools designed to connect us sometimes substitute activity for connection. A message, a reaction, or a new contact can feel like progress without creating the familiarity and mutual understanding that make relationships meaningful.

I've felt this at networking events. You spend an evening introducing yourself, describing what you do, exchanging details, and moving on to the next conversation. Sometimes you meet someone wonderful. Other times you leave with a dozen contacts and the peculiar feeling that you barely met anybody.

It isn't that people are insincere. Often everyone is simply following the same script, trying to make the most of a limited evening. But the best conversations tend to happen when the script disappears.

People want to know the people behind the work

Deloitte’s 2025 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey, covering more than 23,000 respondents across 44 countries, found that 89% of Gen Z and 92% of millennials considered a sense of purpose important to job satisfaction and well-being. Only 6% identified reaching a leadership position as their primary career goal.

[Source: Deloitte, 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey]

Those figures don't mean everybody wants the same things. They do suggest that the familiar corporate vocabulary of status, hierarchy, and advancement doesn't tell the whole story of what motivates people.

The same applies to the companies we choose to support. Edelman’s 2025 Brand Trust research found that 84% of respondents trusted friends and family for accurate brand information and 80% trusted customers like themselves, compared with 58% who trusted brand CEOs. These are survey findings, not proof that personal relationships always outweigh product quality. But they help explain why credibility so often travels through people rather than polished corporate messaging.

[Source: Edelman, 2025 Brand Trust Report]

Think about a small business you return to even though a competitor is cheaper. Perhaps the owner remembers you. Perhaps someone took responsibility when an order went wrong. Perhaps you've watched the people behind the business put real effort into something they care about. Those experiences create a kind of confidence no slogan can manufacture on demand.

Building together beats collecting contacts

One reason I like the idea of building in public is that it gives people something more substantial to connect around. Hackathons, makerspaces, open-source communities, and collaborative projects allow us to get to know one another through decisions, frustrations, small victories, and the occasional spectacular mistake.

It's a different experience from asking someone what they do and immediately calculating whether there might be a business opportunity. When you work alongside people, you discover how they think, whether they listen, and what they do when things get difficult. Trust develops through repeated experience.

That matters in business, especially in consulting, enterprise technology, and sales. A customer may appreciate a polished proposal, but they also want to know who will answer the phone when something goes wrong. They want to feel understood. They want some reason to believe the promises being made.

Where AI actually helps

I am enthusiastic about AI precisely because it can give us more time and better information to work with. It can prepare us for meetings, surface a forgotten conversation, organize commitments, suggest useful introductions, and help us follow through. Those are valuable capabilities.

What I don't want is for the technology to become a substitute for knowing someone. An automated message may be perfectly written and still be completely empty. A system may know a customer's purchase history without understanding why they were disappointed, what they're worried about, or what would restore their confidence.

The opportunity is to use AI behind the scenes so that the human interaction in front of us becomes more thoughtful. Better preparation. Better listening. Better follow-through. Less time copying information between systems and more time doing something meaningful with it.

Our humanity is the advantage

I wrote separately about the copycat economy and how easily technology can reproduce products and features. This is a different question: what kind of business relationships do we actually want to have?

I think we'll see more founders telling their own stories, more customers asking who is responsible for the work, and more communities forming around shared effort rather than corporate messaging. Not because every company needs a charismatic personality at the top, but because people want to know who they're dealing with.

We should be building technology that helps people understand one another, not placing another layer of automation between them. If AI can help us remember, prepare, and follow through while leaving room for genuine conversation, that's progress I can get excited about.

After all, the most valuable connection in a business relationship is still the one between two human beings.
