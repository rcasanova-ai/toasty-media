AI: The Human Advantage

Why trust, reputation, and relationships may become our greatest competitive advantage.

I’ve been thinking about this quite a bit lately, partly because of the projects I’m building, but also because of some broader changes in how people work, socialize, and make purchasing decisions. I think we’re witnessing two significant shifts happening simultaneously. Technology is making it easier than ever to create and replicate products, while culturally, people seem increasingly interested in the individuals and communities behind the things they support.

Those two developments might appear unrelated, but I suspect their intersection will reshape how businesses are built, marketed, and ultimately valued.

We want to know who we’re dealing with

Something has been changing culturally for a while now, particularly among younger generations. There’s a growing emphasis on purpose, personal connection, authenticity, and the people we choose to associate with, both professionally and personally.

Deloitte’s 2025 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey, which included more than 23,000 respondents across 44 countries, found that 89% of Gen Z and 92% of millennials consider having a sense of purpose important to their job satisfaction and well-being. Interestingly, only 6% identified reaching a leadership position as their primary career goal. The traditional markers of professional success aren’t disappearing, but they’re clearly being reconsidered.

[Source: Deloitte, 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey]

Now consider what’s happened with social media over the past fifteen years. For all the garbage it’s brought into our lives, and there’s certainly no shortage of that, one of its more positive contributions has been giving ordinary people the ability to build relationships with audiences who genuinely appreciate what they’re doing.

We’ve watched independent creators develop communities around their personalities, small businesses attract loyal customers because people identify with their founders, and entrepreneurs build followings long before they have anything commercially available. People aren’t necessarily supporting these individuals because they have the best product, the lowest price, or the most sophisticated technology. Often, they’re supporting them because they like them, identify with their experiences, or believe in what they’re trying to accomplish.

Edelman’s 2025 Brand Trust research offers some interesting context here. It found that 84% of respondents trust friends and family for accurate brand information, while 80% trust customers like themselves. Trust in brand CEOs was considerably lower, at 58%. These figures don’t prove that consumers universally prefer personalities over companies, but they do illustrate the importance of peer relationships and personal credibility in how trust is established.

[Source: Edelman, 2025 Brand Trust Report]

I think this helps explain why people increasingly want to understand who’s behind a business. They want to know what motivates someone, what they believe in, and whether they’re the kind of person they’d enjoy dealing with. There’s an emotional dimension to commerce that corporate branding has spent decades trying to manufacture, often with limited success.

Building in public and the return of human connection

Building in public isn’t new. Founders have been documenting their entrepreneurial journeys for years, open-source communities have long embraced transparent development, and collaborative building spaces have existed in various forms around the world for decades.

Hackerspaces, makerspaces, startup communities, hackathons, and more recently the growing number of AI-focused build stations and collaborative development events all reflect a similar idea: people benefit from creating things together, sharing knowledge, and being part of a community.

I’ve attended enough events over the years to know that some of these initiatives are considerably better than others. Some create genuine opportunities for collaboration, while others are little more than networking exercises dressed up as innovation. But when they work, there’s something valuable happening that goes well beyond the technology being developed.

I’ve also watched the nature of these gatherings change over time, and I wonder whether some of that change reflects a broader problem with how we connect. You can spend an entire evening moving from one conversation to another, answering the same questions: “What do you do?” or “What are you building?” There’s nothing inherently wrong with those questions. They’re natural starting points, and when you’re trying to grow a business or find collaborators, they’re important conversations to have. But after a dozen or more of them, you can leave an event with a scattered collection of names, projects, and LinkedIn connections without feeling like you’ve actually connected with anyone.

There’s a strange tension in that experience. You attend because you want to meet people, discover opportunities, and be part of a community, yet somewhere along the way, the pressure to network can overshadow the very human connection that brought you there. You find yourself balancing the desire to have a genuine conversation with the feeling that you should be talking about your projects, making introductions, or finding the next opportunity. I’ve certainly felt that conflict, and I suspect I’m not alone.

The World Health Organization’s 2025 Commission on Social Connection reported that approximately one in six people globally experiences loneliness, with the figure rising to roughly one in five among adolescents and young adults. These are significant numbers, particularly when we consider how much technology has supposedly improved our ability to communicate.

[Source: World Health Organization, Commission on Social Connection, 2025]

I don’t think startup events or building communities are going to solve loneliness, but I do believe there’s a genuine appetite for environments where people can spend time together doing something meaningful, rather than simply exchanging introductions and moving on. There’s a different kind of connection that develops when people collaborate, solve problems, share frustrations, and celebrate progress together. You get to know someone through their actions and experiences, rather than the thirty-second explanation of what they do for a living.

Building in public can extend that experience beyond a physical location. When you share the process of creating a business, people begin to understand the decisions you’re making and the challenges you’re facing. They see the mistakes, the changes in direction, and the occasional moments when something finally works. They also get to know the people contributing along the way, creating opportunities for relationships that develop naturally through shared interests and experiences.

Perhaps that’s part of what we’ve been missing. More opportunities to actually get to know the people behind the projects, rather than just the projects themselves.

We should start putting people back into business

Look at how many corporate websites have evolved over the years. We have pages describing solutions, capabilities, industries served, and carefully worded value propositions. There are endless references to innovation, transformation, and customer-centric approaches, often accompanied by stock photography of people smiling around conference tables.

Yet in many cases, it’s surprisingly difficult to find out who actually runs the company.

I think we’re going to see more companies reconsider that approach. If I’m considering working with a business, I want to understand the people responsible for delivering what they’re promising. I want to know their experience, what motivates them, and how they think. If something goes wrong, I want some confidence that I’m dealing with people who will take responsibility and work toward a solution.

That’s particularly relevant in professional services, consulting, enterprise technology, and other industries where the relationship often matters as much as the underlying product.

There’s also no shortage of predictions about AI agents replacing salespeople, managing customer relationships, negotiating contracts, and eventually conducting entire commercial transactions without human involvement. Some of those predictions will undoubtedly prove accurate. AI is already taking over administrative work, prospecting research, scheduling, reporting, and various other tasks that have consumed enormous amounts of time in traditional sales organizations.

But after spending much of my professional life in sales, business development, and relationship management, I have a difficult time accepting the idea that human relationships are simply an inefficiency waiting to be eliminated.

Anyone who has spent meaningful time in sales understands that purchasing decisions aren’t always made according to a perfectly rational assessment of features, pricing, and performance. People make decisions based on confidence, familiarity, experience, perceived risk, and whether they believe the person sitting across from them understands their situation.

Sometimes two companies offer nearly identical products, and the deciding factor is simply that the customer likes and trusts one person more than the other.

That’s human behavior, and I don’t see it disappearing because we’ve developed software capable of conducting a convincing conversation. In fact, I suspect that as AI-generated communication becomes increasingly common, genuine human interaction may become more valuable in certain contexts.

When every company can produce polished proposals, personalized outreach, sophisticated presentations, and compelling marketing materials almost instantly, those things become less effective as signals of credibility. What becomes harder to manufacture is a meaningful history with another person.

Hello, storytelling.

Your story might be your greatest competitive advantage

I think storytelling is going to become one of the more important capabilities for entrepreneurs and business leaders in the years ahead. I don’t mean hiring someone to manufacture an inspirational founder narrative or asking an AI system to turn your LinkedIn profile into a heroic account of overcoming adversity. We’ve already got more than enough of that.

I’m talking about understanding your own experiences well enough to communicate why you do what you do, how you arrived at your conclusions, and what you’ve learned along the way.

Every entrepreneur has a story, although not everyone recognizes which parts are worth telling. Sometimes the most compelling elements aren’t the successful product launches or impressive accomplishments. They’re the decisions that didn’t work, the opportunities that fell apart, the relationships that changed someone’s direction, or the circumstances that led them to pursue an idea in the first place.

These experiences shape how people approach problems, make decisions, and interact with others. They’re also what make one founder different from another, even when both are working on remarkably similar products.

As AI continues to lower the barriers to creating products, I suspect the things that distinguish one business from another will increasingly have less to do with what it can build and more to do with the people building it. Features can be reproduced, interfaces can be imitated, and technical advantages can disappear remarkably quickly. But relationships take time to develop. Trust has to be earned. Reputation is built through experience, and the story behind a business is shaped by decisions, successes, failures, and the people involved along the way.

Perhaps the most valuable thing we can do in an increasingly automated world is make ourselves a little more visible. Share what we're building, explain why it matters, acknowledge what we've learned, and give people an opportunity to know who they're dealing with. Because when everyone has access to the same extraordinary technology, our greatest competitive advantage might turn out to be something we've had all along.

Our humanity.
