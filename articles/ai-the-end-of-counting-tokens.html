AI: The End of Counting Tokens

What unlimited mobile data can teach us about the future economics of artificial intelligence.

I have spent most of my career around technologies that get interesting once they become cheap enough, connected enough and used widely enough to change behaviour.

In mobile, that meant watching data move from something people carefully rationed to something they barely think about. Along the way came M2M, IoT, big data, machine learning and increasingly automated systems. AI feels less like a sudden break from that history and more like the next acceleration of it.

What feels familiar is the economics. As the cost of using a technology falls, people usually do not just pocket the savings. They find more ways to use it. Mobile data became cheaper, networks became faster, and consumption kept climbing. Customers often continued spending roughly the same amount, but what they received for that money changed dramatically.

I see that in my own phone bill. Today in Thailand I pay roughly 400 baht a month for effectively unlimited mobile data. There is a speed cap, but in normal use I rarely notice it. In Canada I was getting robbed by paying around CAD $130 a month for a high data plan that still had a limit. (While the Telcos report record profits and layoff entire swaths of people). I am consuming vastly more mobile data today than I would have twenty years ago, but I do not think about the individual gigabyte anymore because the unit has stopped being particularly relevant to me.

That experience makes me skeptical of the assumption that dramatically cheaper inference necessarily means people will spend dramatically less on AI. Mobile suggests that consumers and businesses may continue spending similar amounts, or considerably more, while receiving orders of magnitude more capability for that money. As the cost of a megabyte fell, we did not limit ourselves to sending cheaper emails. We started streaming movies over the same network.

When tokens become background infrastructure

AI is likely to follow a similar path. We still talk about tokens because tokens are currently visible economically. Developers compare API pricing, companies decide whether a particular workload justifies an expensive model, and people hit usage limits and notice when they have moved into a more expensive tier. If inference costs continue falling at anything close to their recent pace, much of that will eventually become background infrastructure and the practical question will shift from how many tokens we can afford to how much useful work we can reasonably ask a machine to do.

That lack of friction has been central to mass adoption in mobile as well. Nobody opens TikTok and thinks about TCP/IP, radio spectrum, content delivery networks, video codecs or the price per gigabyte required to make the experience possible. People tapping a phone at a payment terminal generally do not care which clearing systems were involved either. The underlying technologies became sufficiently reliable, inexpensive and integrated that the complexity could disappear underneath the product.

Commoditization in this context does not mean that economic value disappears. It changes where that value can be captured. Bandwidth became dramatically cheaper, but cheap bandwidth enabled smartphones, streaming, cloud software, social networks, ride sharing and entire categories of businesses that would have been impossible when every megabyte was expensive. Cloud computing made access to computing infrastructure easier and cheaper while creating an enormous market for the software and services built on top of it.

If generic inference follows the same path, having access to a capable model will gradually provide less differentiation. More of the economic value can move into systems that combine intelligence with something harder to reproduce, whether that is physical assets, specialized sensors, trusted data, distribution, regulatory permission, proprietary workflows, access to people, reliable execution or the ability to operate safely in the physical world.

A model answering a question about a factory is relatively inexpensive intelligence. A system that understands the factory, sees what is happening on the floor, predicts a problem, identifies the required component, purchases it, schedules the repair, coordinates with the technician and safely adjusts production until the repair is completed is a much more valuable proposition. The underlying inference may become extremely cheap without making the complete system cheap, because the value has moved into everything required to turn intelligence into a reliable outcome.

I started this with my 400 baht mobile plan because there is something wonderfully boring about unlimited data. Twenty years ago, the idea that I could carry a connection this capable everywhere, use as much of it as I wanted and barely think about the network would have sounded extraordinary. Today I complain when the elevator kills my signal for thirty seconds. Technology has a funny way of becoming most consequential just as we stop being impressed by it. If AI and inference follow the same path, perhaps the real milestone will be when we stop talking about tokens quite so much.
