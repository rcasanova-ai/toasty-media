When Machines Start Thinking for Themselves

How sensors, edge computing, robotics and next-generation networks are bringing AI into the physical world.

The comparison between AI and mobile extends beyond pricing because mobile did not develop in isolation. Once networks covered large populations, we began connecting things other than people. M2M connected machines and IoT dramatically expanded the number and variety of connected devices. Those devices produced enormous amounts of data, which helped drive investment in big data infrastructure, while machine learning gave us better ways to extract patterns and predictions from it. Generative and agentic AI are building on that progression rather than replacing it.

For years we have been putting sensors into the physical world and teaching software to interpret what those sensors produce. Cameras, microphones, GPS, accelerometers, industrial sensors, radar, wearables, vehicles and connected machines continuously create information about their surroundings. What is changing is the range of things software can reasonably do with that information.

A temperature sensor attached to a machine might once have generated a reading that an application compared with a threshold to trigger an alert. With enough historical data, machine learning could estimate the probability of failure. Increasingly, a system can combine that same reading with vibration data, maintenance records, images, operating conditions, inventory, production schedules and information from other machines, then use that context to determine what needs to happen next and potentially initiate the action itself.

Intelligence that cannot afford to wait

This takes AI beyond something we visit in a browser and into the physical world, where the constraints are different. An extra second waiting for a chatbot response is annoying, while an extra second for a robot to react to someone walking in front of it can be dangerous. Sending every observation to a large model in a distant data centre and waiting for an answer therefore will not make sense for every application.

As models become smaller, hardware becomes more capable and inference becomes cheaper, more intelligence can run close to the sensor. Some decisions will happen directly on the device, others at the network edge, and workloads that genuinely need enormous models can still go to the cloud. For many physical applications, the objective is effectively zero perceptible latency, where the sensor observes something, the system understands it and the machine responds quickly enough that these feel like parts of the same process.

We have been experimenting with some of these ideas ourselves. What interests me about something like that is not putting a chatbot inside a furry robot. It is what becomes possible when cameras, microphones and other sensors give a machine continuous context about the environment around it, while local inference handles what it can immediately and the network provides additional capability when it is actually needed.

That same combination of sensing, inference and action changes where economic value sits. Generic inference will become increasingly difficult to charge a large premium for on its own, while systems that combine inference with the right sensors, proprietary context, software, permissions and physical equipment can solve considerably more valuable problems. There is a very large difference between generating a paragraph about warehouse efficiency and operating a warehouse more efficiently.

From 5G to 6G

The evolution from 5G toward 6G makes that relationship between connectivity and physical intelligence more interesting. The useful part of 6G is not simply another increase in the number beside the signal bars on a phone. Work around AI-native network architectures, integrated sensing and communications, distributed computing and much more precise positioning points toward networks that participate more directly in the systems using them.

A wireless network in that environment is not necessarily limited to transporting information from a sensor to an application. It can contribute to sensing and localization, provide nearby computing resources and support extremely low-latency applications involving vehicles, robots, industrial equipment and other autonomous systems. Communications, sensing, computation and intelligence begin to share the same infrastructure rather than operating as completely separate layers.

We are also starting to see parts of this progression move into consumer hardware. Meta’s work around persistent AI in wearable devices is interesting less because any particular product is guaranteed to succeed and more because of the direction it represents. AI that has persistent access to cameras, microphones and other sensors has a fundamentally different relationship with the user and the surrounding environment than an application waiting for someone to type a prompt.

Mobile gave us increasingly abundant connectivity, M2M and IoT expanded that connectivity to machines and sensors, big data gave us the infrastructure to handle what those systems produced, and machine learning helped turn that information into prediction. AI extends what software can interpret and decide, while edge computing brings more of that capability closer to where events occur. Robotics gives those decisions a way to affect the physical world, and 6G is developing toward a tighter relationship between communications, sensing and computation.

These technologies have mostly been discussed as separate markets because they developed at different speeds and were sold by different industries, but those boundaries become much less meaningful when they are assembled into a product. What interests me is what becomes practical to build when sensing and intelligence become ordinary infrastructure rather than expensive features that have to be justified individually.
